Abstract

What this project is testing

Digital accent archives make it possible to hear speech variation at a scale that was previously difficult to access, but interfaces can also encourage a false shortcut: treating one speaker as “the accent” of a country, language or social group. This project asks how a research interface can support accent learning and comparative phonetic observation while preserving source provenance, within-group variation and uncertainty.

The work combines two layers: a research protocol for speaker-level coding and a supporting prototype for discovery, A/B listening, practice-session design and independent annotation. The current version is exploratory. It does not claim representative coverage, validated pedagogical effects or a finished accent taxonomy.

Core claim under test: an interface that foregrounds provenance, speaker-level metadata, uncertainty and independent coding can make accent comparison more useful without requiring users to collapse variation into national labels.
01 · Research question

Primary question

How can a source-grounded digital interface organize global English accent evidence for learnability and comparison without collapsing speaker-level variation into stereotypes?

Secondary questions

  • Which phonetic descriptors can be coded consistently enough to support comparison across independent reviewers?
  • Which observations should remain qualitative rather than be forced into categories?
  • How should provenance and uncertainty be shown so users can distinguish source facts from researcher interpretation?
  • Can a comparison workflow support actors and advanced learners without implying that a single recording represents a population?
02 · Contribution

What is new here

Evidence architecture

Source links, speaker metadata, coding notes and reviewer identity are separate data layers rather than one flattened “accent profile.”

Research / pedagogy split

Learning tools and research annotations coexist, but a coaching drill is not treated as evidence and a research annotation is not presented as a learning outcome.

Disagreement as data

Independent coders can disagree, flag uncertainty and reconcile later; disagreement is not silently overwritten.

Anti-stereotyping constraint

The design explicitly prohibits “one speaker = one place” interpretation and avoids automated accent scores.

03 · Evidence base

Archives and unit of analysis

The prototype is designed around primary or source-adjacent speech archives. Two source families currently inform the architecture:

  • IDEA — International Dialects of English Archive: used as a source archive for individual speaker recordings and associated contextual material. Audio remains at the original source; the research tool should store only the minimum metadata needed for indexing and provenance.
  • George Mason University Speech Accent Archive: useful for controlled comparison because speakers read a common elicitation paragraph and the archive exposes demographic/linguistic context and phonetic analysis.
Unit of analysis: one recorded speaker/sample. Geographic, linguistic or demographic labels are indexing variables—not claims that the speaker represents everyone sharing that label.

Sampling policy for the pilot

The first coded set should be purposeful, not representative: enough variation to stress-test the codebook across source types, language backgrounds and speech patterns. A later quantitative study would need a pre-registered sampling frame before population-level inference.

04 · Method

Research workflow

Index source records

Record stable sample ID, source URL, archive, source-visible speaker/context metadata and access date. Do not infer missing demographics.

Select a pilot corpus

Use transparent inclusion/exclusion rules and preserve the reason each sample entered the coding set.

Independent coding

Coder A and coder B annotate the same sample without seeing each other’s answers. Free-text observations stay distinct from categorical variables.

Quantify agreement where appropriate

Report raw observed agreement and a chance-corrected coefficient such as Cohen’s κ for pre-defined nominal fields when sample size and category distribution make that interpretable.

Reconcile disagreements

Review source audio and operational definitions; preserve original coder responses and store the reconciled decision separately.

Evaluate the interface separately

Usability and learning outcomes require their own study. Prototype activity logs are not evidence of pedagogical effectiveness.

06 · Agreement analysis

How disagreement is reported

The current research module stores annotations separately by coder and can compute observed agreement and Cohen’s κ for selected nominal fields on samples coded by both reviewers. These are different quantities: raw agreement is a descriptive proportion; κ adjusts agreement for expected chance agreement under its model assumptions.

Interpretation rule: no κ value is meaningful without n, category prevalence and the operational codebook. Small pilot sets should be reported as calibration evidence, not as proof that the coding system is reliable in general.

08 · Evaluation plan

What would count as evidence

The next phase should separate method calibration from product evaluation.

Study A — coding calibration

Two independent reviewers code the same pre-selected pilot set. Report completion rate, uncertain items, disagreement patterns, observed agreement and κ only for suitable nominal fields. Revise the codebook before expansion.

Study B — interface usability

Expert/advanced users complete predefined comparison tasks. Capture task success, error patterns, provenance comprehension and qualitative feedback. Do not infer learning gains from satisfaction alone.

Later pedagogical study

If the tool is used for training, a separate prospective design should define a target skill, pre/post measures, comparison condition and assessment procedure before claiming educational benefit.

09 · Ethics & provenance

Guardrails

  • Do not redistribute IDEA audio or long copyrighted source text; link to the original record.
  • Preserve the original archive citation and access date for every indexed item.
  • Do not infer protected or sensitive traits from a voice sample.
  • Do not rank speakers by “good/bad,” “native-like” or an automated accent score.
  • Keep researcher observations distinct from source commentary.
  • Use the minimum speaker metadata necessary for the research question.

The GMU Speech Accent Archive explicitly describes itself as both a teaching and research tool and publishes its licensing information; any redistributed or derived material should follow the source’s current license terms.

10 · Limitations

What the current project cannot claim

  • The indexed archives are not a census of global English variation.
  • Archive contributors are not random samples of the populations attached to their labels.
  • Auditory coding is researcher-dependent and requires operational definitions plus calibration.
  • Lexical-set and rhythm labels are analytical tools; they can oversimplify gradient or mixed patterns.
  • The current build has no completed outcome study, so it cannot claim improved pronunciation, acting performance or reduced bias.
  • Independent expert review has not yet been completed.
11 · Reproducibility

Research record

The tool is designed to export source IDs, per-coder annotations, sessions and activity history as machine-readable files. The public research layer will version the protocol and codebook separately from the application so methodological changes can be tracked.
